Section 1

Overcome drug and substance abuse

Substance-use problems are health conditions, not proof that someone is weak or beyond help. Recovery is possible; a respectful, evidence-based response combines safety, treatment, social support and personally meaningful goals.

Possible effects

  • Brain and mental health: impaired attention, memory, judgement and coordination; anxiety, low mood, sleep problems or worsening of existing mental-health symptoms.
  • Dependence and withdrawal: cravings, tolerance, difficulty controlling use and withdrawal symptoms. Alcohol or sedative withdrawal can be medically dangerous.
  • Physical health: risks vary by substance and may include heart, liver, lung or other organ damage, infections, injuries and overdose.
  • Education and work: missed classes, reduced concentration, lower performance and lost opportunities.
  • Relationships and finances: conflict, isolation, spending pressure and reduced trust.
  • Safety and law: accidents, unsafe decisions and possible legal consequences.

Effects differ by substance, dose, frequency, age, health and context. Not every effect occurs in every person.

Practical ways to overcome it

  1. Tell a safe person. Choose a trusted adult, health worker, counsellor, pastor or supportive family member.
  2. Get assessed. A clinician can help identify risks, co-occurring conditions and suitable treatment.
  3. Make a safety plan. Seek urgent care for overdose, severe confusion, seizures, unconsciousness or breathing problems.
  4. Use professional treatment. Options may include counselling, behavioural therapies, peer support and, for some conditions, prescribed medicines.
  5. Reduce triggers. Plan how to handle pressure, places, contacts and emotions linked to use.
  6. Build a recovery routine. Regular sleep, food, physical activity, meaningful activity and supportive relationships help overall wellbeing.
  7. Plan for setbacks. A lapse is a reason to reconnect with support, not a reason to give up.
  8. Support, don't shame. Families can set clear boundaries while treating the person with dignity.
Safety note: Do not attempt to detoxify someone at home if they may be dependent on alcohol, benzodiazepines or other sedatives. Medical supervision may be needed. Do not mix unknown substances or take medicines not prescribed to you.

Know your career

Career choice is a process of exploring interests, abilities, values, opportunities and lived experience — not a one-time test. This short questionnaire uses Holland’s RIASEC interest framework as a reflection tool, not a validated psychological test or a guarantee of job fit.

Career interest questionnaire

Rate each activity from 0 (not interested) to 4 (very interested). Answer for what you genuinely enjoy, not what others expect.

Turn a result into a plan

  1. Choose the top two or three interest themes, then identify what you can actually do well and what you want to learn.
  2. List your values: service, income, stability, creativity, independence, teamwork or flexibility.
  3. Research at least three occupations using official training-provider, professional-body and labour-market information.
  4. Interview a person doing the work; ask about daily tasks, entry requirements, costs and challenges.
  5. Try a small project, volunteer role, job shadow or introductory course before committing.
  6. For Kenya, verify current entry requirements with the relevant institution and professional regulator; requirements and job markets change.

Child discipline: guidance with love and consistency

Healthy discipline teaches self-control, responsibility and repair; it is not humiliation, intimidation or violence. Children need firm, age-appropriate limits alongside warmth, safety and opportunities to practise good choices.

Ellen G. White writes in Child Guidance, “Let parents be models of truthfulness” (chapter 28, p. 150). The surrounding passage stresses that adults’ examples and harsh responses influence whether children feel safe telling the truth. Read the original chapter.

20 practical principles for child discipline

  1. Connect before correcting. Get the child’s attention calmly and respectfully.
  2. Set a few clear rules. Explain what the rule protects and what behaviour is expected.
  3. Be consistent. Apply rules fairly and avoid consequences that depend on adult mood.
  4. Model what you ask for. Adults should demonstrate honesty, courtesy and self-control.
  5. Use natural or logical consequences. Make consequences related, reasonable and respectful.
  6. Notice good choices. Praise effort, truth-telling, kindness and responsibility specifically.
  7. Teach the replacement behaviour. Show how to ask, wait, apologise or solve a disagreement.
  8. Listen before deciding. Ask what happened and hear each child where appropriate.
  9. Regulate yourself first. Pause if angry; return when you can speak safely.
  10. Never use humiliation. Avoid insults, threats, ridicule and comparing siblings.
  1. Protect physical safety. Do not shake, beat, burn or otherwise injure a child.
  2. Respect developmental stages. Expectations should match age, ability, disability and circumstances.
  3. Give limited choices. Two acceptable choices build agency within boundaries.
  4. Teach repair. Help the child restore what was damaged, apologise meaningfully or make amends.
  5. Make routines predictable. Sleep, meals, homework and chores are easier with structure.
  6. Encourage industry. Give manageable, useful tasks; teach skills patiently and celebrate effort, not perfection.
  7. Make work meaningful. Let children help with age-appropriate household, school, garden or community duties.
  8. Support honesty. Thank children for telling the truth, especially when it is difficult; still address the behaviour.
  9. Coordinate caregivers. Agree on rules and avoid undermining one another in front of children.
  10. Seek help when needed. Repeated severe aggression, fear, trauma or developmental concerns may warrant a paediatrician, counsellor or child-protection service.

Encouraging industry and useful work

In Child Guidance, White discusses training children through useful duties and the development of responsibility. Read the chapter in context and apply it without placing adult burdens on children.

  • Teach one task at a time: putting away toys, making a bed, preparing school items or helping safely with meals.
  • Demonstrate, do the task together, then allow independent practice.
  • Use chores to build contribution and belonging, not as a threat or a substitute for affection.
  • Match tasks to age, safety and ability; provide tools and supervision.
  • Recognise persistence, care and improvement rather than demanding perfection.
  • Balance duties with play, rest, school, friendships and emotional support.

Reference: Ellen G. White, Child Guidance, sections on useful employment, habits and character training. Search/read the complete book.

Honesty: why children may lie, and how adults can respond

Possible causes

  • Fear of severe punishment, shouting or humiliation.
  • Copying adults or peers who model dishonesty.
  • Trying to avoid consequences or gain approval.
  • Imagination, immature understanding or confusion between fantasy and fact in younger children.
  • Pressure to meet unrealistic expectations or hide a mistake.
  • Impulsivity, shame, stress or a learned habit; context matters.

What helps

  • Stay calm and ask neutral questions instead of setting traps.
  • Separate the child’s worth from the behaviour: “I love you; we need to tell the truth.”
  • Make honesty safer than concealment while maintaining fair consequences.
  • Model admitting mistakes and correcting them openly.
  • Reward truth-telling and help repair the harm caused.
  • Explore underlying fear, bullying, learning difficulty or unmet needs if the pattern persists.
In Child Guidance, chapter 29, White says honesty should be “practiced and taught” (CG 152). This short phrase is quoted for discussion; consult the full chapter for context: Honesty and Integrity. Her counsel is religious guidance, not a substitute for current child-development evidence or safeguarding standards.

Family wellbeing

Strong families are not conflict-free; they practise repair, respect, safety and shared responsibility. Faith can be a source of meaning when it is expressed with compassion and never used to excuse abuse.

The Adventist Family Ministries statement describes family members as helping one another toward maturity, and cites Ellen G. White: “The well-being of society ... depend[s] upon home influences” (The Ministry of Healing, p. 349). Read the Adventist statement.

18 practices for a healthier family

  1. Make emotional safety a priority. No threats, intimidation or violence.
  2. Listen to understand. Let each person finish before responding.
  3. Speak respectfully. Address actions and needs, not a person’s worth.
  4. Schedule connection. Create regular meals, walks, worship or check-ins.
  5. Share responsibilities. Allocate household work fairly and age-appropriately.
  6. Keep promises. If a plan changes, explain and repair trust.
  7. Use “I” statements. Describe feelings and needs without blame.
  8. Pause heated conflict. Agree on a time to return to the discussion.
  9. Apologise sincerely. Name the harm, take responsibility and change behaviour.
  1. Protect couple time. Discuss practical matters as well as hopes and affection.
  2. Respect privacy and boundaries. Ask before sharing personal stories or messages.
  3. Support children’s voices. Invite opinions while adults retain safe responsibility.
  4. Plan money together. Discuss priorities, needs, savings and debt without secrecy or shame.
  5. Make space for grief. People process loss and stress differently.
  6. Care for caregivers. Encourage rest, friendships and appropriate support.
  7. Use faith gently. Pray or worship together when welcome; never force disclosure or silence a victim.
  8. Seek outside help early. A qualified counsellor, health professional or trusted support service may help.
  9. Keep safety first. Where abuse occurs, seek a safe plan and professional/local support; joint counselling may not be safe in coercive situations.

Family reflection questions

  • What helps each person feel respected and heard at home?
  • Which recurring conflict could be improved by a clearer agreement?
  • What small shared activity can we protect each week?
  • When trust is damaged, what specific action would help rebuild it?
  • Who can we contact if stress, addiction, violence or mental-health concerns become too much?

How to study effectively

Good studying is active, spaced, purposeful and matched to the learner. Re-reading alone can feel familiar without proving that you can recall or apply the material.

Study principles for all ages

  1. Set a clear goal. Define what you should explain, solve, create or remember at the end.
  2. Use active recall. Close the book and retrieve the idea from memory; answer practice questions.
  3. Space your practice. Revisit material across days rather than cramming in one sitting.
  4. Interleave related problems. Mix question types after learning the basic method.
  5. Teach it simply. Explain a concept aloud as if teaching someone else.
  6. Use feedback. Mark practice, identify errors and retry without looking at the solution.
  7. Protect sleep. Sleep supports attention and memory; avoid all-night cramming.
  8. Manage distractions. Keep the phone away during focused blocks and take planned breaks.
  9. Study in short, focused sessions. Adapt session length to age, task and attention.
  10. Ask for help early. Clarify confusing foundations before moving to harder work.

Sample focused session

  1. 5 minutes: write the goal and recall yesterday’s lesson.
  2. 20–30 minutes: learn or solve one defined topic.
  3. 5 minutes: break, stretch and drink water.
  4. 10 minutes: answer questions without notes.
  5. 5 minutes: correct mistakes and plan the next review.

This is a flexible example, not a fixed rule. Younger learners may need shorter periods and adult support.

Adapt study methods by age group

Early childhood (about 3–6)

  • Learn through play, stories, songs, movement and real objects.
  • Keep activities brief and switch tasks before fatigue.
  • Read aloud, ask simple questions and invite the child to explain what happened.
  • Celebrate curiosity and effort; protect play and sleep.

Primary school (about 6–12)

  • Use visual schedules, flashcards, drawing, manipulatives and short quizzes.
  • Practise reading aloud, number facts and explaining solutions.
  • Build a consistent homework routine with adult guidance, then gradually increase independence.
  • Give specific feedback and praise strategies, not only scores.

Teenagers (about 13–18)

  • Use a weekly timetable with realistic priorities and breaks.
  • Combine retrieval practice, spaced revision, worked examples and past papers.
  • For each error, record why it happened and how to avoid it next time.
  • Balance schoolwork with sleep, exercise, friends and emotional wellbeing.

College, university and adults

  • Break assignments into milestones and begin before the deadline.
  • Read critically: identify claims, evidence, assumptions and source quality.
  • Use project-based practice, peer discussion and real-world application.
  • Schedule revision around work, family and energy; revisit difficult concepts repeatedly.

Study by subject area

🔬 Science and mathematics

  • Understand concepts before memorising definitions.
  • Practise calculations and explain each step; draw diagrams and label processes.
  • Use experiments, simulations and error analysis where safe and available.
  • Make a formula sheet from memory, then check it.

📚 Humanities

For history, literature, philosophy and languages:

  • Build timelines, themes, character maps and vocabulary sets.
  • Support interpretations with specific evidence or quotations.
  • Practise structured essays: claim, evidence, explanation and conclusion.
  • Compare viewpoints and learn context, not isolated facts alone.

🌍 Social sciences

For geography, economics, sociology and related fields:

  • Define terms and connect theories to real examples.
  • Interpret tables, maps, graphs and data carefully.
  • Compare causes, consequences and alternative explanations.
  • Practise evaluating evidence, limitations and bias.

🎨 Artistic and creative subjects

  • Practise deliberately in short, regular sessions.
  • Keep a sketchbook, rehearsal log or portfolio.
  • Study examples, then create your own work and reflect on choices.
  • Seek feedback on one specific element at a time.

🔧 Technical and vocational subjects

  • Learn safety rules and demonstrate procedures before working independently.
  • Practise using checklists and real or simulated tasks.
  • Keep a troubleshooting journal: symptom, possible cause, test, result.
  • Build projects, document steps and explain why a method works.

💻 ICT and computing

  • Type code yourself; do not only watch tutorials.
  • Build small projects and test one feature at a time.
  • Read error messages, isolate the issue and document the fix.
  • Use spaced recall for concepts and hands-on repetition for tools.
